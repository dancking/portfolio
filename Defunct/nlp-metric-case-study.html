Building a metric for meaning change

There was no ruler for how far a word's meaning moves when someone paraphrases it. I built one from word embeddings, then tested it every way I could: against a known result, an independent human benchmark, blind qualitative coding and the confound most likely to fool it.

¶ How I work

What this case study shows about how I work

  • I define what a metric has to do before choosing how to build it. The failures of earlier human methods became a list of requirements.
  • I choose instruments that can't be tuned toward the answer. Off-the-shelf embeddings meant no free parameters to adjust until the results looked right.
  • I validate against independent evidence, not just face validity. A known result, a human benchmark and blind qualitative coding all had to agree.
  • I hunt for confounds and state limits plainly. I tested the explanation most likely to fool the metric and said where it still falls short.

§ The measurement problem

How do you measure how far a word's meaning moved?

The research needed to know how much the noun and the verb each changed when people reinterpreted odd sentences. But paraphrases blend the two, and there's no ground truth to check against.

An example from the paper
The car limped→The badly functioning vehicle struggled to drive
from the nounfrom the verbfrom both? Judges couldn't agree

Which part came from car and which from limped? Every earlier method depended on human judges answering questions like this.

Human method 1

Divide and rate

Judges split each paraphrase into noun and verb parts, then rate each part's similarity to the original word.

✕ Slow, and judges often couldn't agree on the split, so much of the data was lost.
Human method 2

Retrace

New judges read the paraphrase and pick the original word from a list.

✕ Tied to the specific word lists, which weren't built to measure how much meaning changed.
Human method 3

Double paraphrase

New people paraphrase the paraphrase. If the original word comes back, its meaning survived.

Nouns19%
Verbs4%
✕ Objective, but most responses give no signal: few words ever resurfaced.
So the new metric had to be
✓No splitting ✓Graded, not yes/no ✓Every response scored ✓No judgment calls ✓Scalable and replicable
” Definition and construction

Meaning retained: how close the paraphrase stays to the original word

Word embeddings place words that appear in similar contexts close together. I scored each paraphrase by comparing it, as a whole, to the original noun and to the original verb.

1

Paraphrase

One participant's free-text response.

The candle flickered
2

Clean

Keep words in the model's 3M-word vocabulary; function words like “the” drop out.

The candle flickered
3

Embed

Look up each word's normalized 300-dimension vector (Google News, 100B words).

4

Compose

Average the word vectors into a single vector for the whole paraphrase.

candle + flickered ÷ 2
5

Compare

Cosine similarity with the original noun and with the original verb.

lantern .47 · limped .22
Meaning retained=cos(original word,whole paraphrase)
Change signal=noun score−verb score

Scored per response. A positive signal means the verb's meaning moved further than the noun's.

Worked example from the paper
The lantern limped → “The candle flickered”
lantern ↔ paraphrase.47
limped ↔ paraphrase.22

Change signal = .47 − .22 = +.25: the verb moved further.

Design decision
Why
Compare each word to the whole paraphrase
Removes the splitting step that caused disagreement and data loss.
Normalize every vector
Puts noun and verb on equal footing: against their own original sentence they score identically, so any gap comes from the paraphrase.
Use pretrained vectors, no training
No free parameters to tune toward the expected result, and anyone can replicate the scores.
word2vec over GloVe and LSA
Among the closest matches to human similarity judgments across 17 benchmark datasets, and the most widely used.
Average words into one vector
The standard approach, which does as well as more complex ones. The known cost: it ignores word order.
Model the per-response signal with mixed effects
Each response is its own control. Random effects for people and items keep the inference honest.

1Replication · Experiment 1 · 109 people, 526 paraphrases

First, can it find what humans already found?

In 1988, human judges showed that verbs change meaning more than nouns, especially in strained sentences. I reran part of that study with the metric in place of the judges.

Then, does an independent human measure agree?

Embeddings have known blind spots, so I re-measured Experiment 2 with no algorithm at all. New people paraphrased the paraphrases, and I counted how often the original words came back: lossy, but objective.

What the metric says

As sentences get stranger, verb similarity falls (p = .001) while noun similarity stays flat (p = .65).

What people say, with no algorithm

In the human task, verbs came back less often as strain rose (p = .02). Nouns didn't move (p = .69). Different instrument, same shape.

Nouns came back 2× as often: 21% vs. 10%

Even where embeddings are weakest

Embeddings are known to be less accurate for words with many meanings. Humans showed the same polysemy effect: many-meaning words changed more in both (humans: verbs p < .01, nouns marginal at p = .06).

4Construct validity · 576 blind-coded paraphrases

Do low scores mean what they should?

Two linguistics coders, blind to the hypotheses, labeled how each word changed (initial κ = .58, then full consensus). If the metric is valid, the paraphrases it scores as most changed should be the ones people coded as the biggest leaps, and for verbs those leaps are metaphors.

5Confounds & robustness · Experiments 1 and 2

Could something other than meaning explain the scores?

The likeliest culprit: strained sentences might produce longer paraphrases, and averaging more words could drag similarity down on its own.

Paraphrase length barely changed with strain
Mean words per paraphrase (excluding words the model drops) for the least- and most-strained sentence
Least-strained sentence Most-strained sentence
Length as a covariate: verb strain effect −.29 → −.28. Nearly identical. Wrong direction to explain it: in Exp 2, longer paraphrases had slightly higher verb scores. Exclusions restored: rerunning with every mechanical and noncompliant response put back gave the same conclusions. Hardest items: the sentences with the most exclusions showed the same pattern (Exp 1: verbs .20 vs. nouns .32).
“ What the metric made possible

The question the metric was built to answer

With a validated metric, the paper could test why verbs bend: whether it's because they have more stored meanings, or because readers adjust them on the spot.

Verbs bend

Under strain, verb meaning moves and noun meaning holds, in every experiment.

Polysemy ruled out

Many-meaning words shift the baseline but don't bend faster (interaction p = .78).

6× metaphor

Verbs changed through metaphor six times as often as nouns (165 vs. 27).

§ Limits, stated plainly

What the metric can't tell you, and what I'd do next

Limits
  • It's a similarity score in a news-trained space, not a model of how people represent meaning. The paper says so explicitly.
  • Embeddings blur similarity with association and are weaker for words with many meanings.
  • Averaging ignores word order.
  • Agreement with humans was shown at the level of effects, not paraphrase by paraphrase.
  • Each stimulus set used six nouns and six verbs.
Next steps
  • Item-level agreement: score a sample with human ratings and report correlation and reliability, not just matching effects.
  • Benchmark newer models: contextual sentence embeddings and LLM raters, tested against the same human data. The data are shared on OSF.
  • Wider coverage: more verbs and nouns, and transitive sentences.
