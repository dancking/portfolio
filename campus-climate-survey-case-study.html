A campus climate survey of 1,300+ students, alumni, faculty and staff, built to show which groups experience the university differently, and on what.

Key strengths

What this case study shows about how I work

  • I build one instrument for many audiences. Role-based wording and branching kept a single survey valid for students, alumni, faculty, staff and administrators.
  • I check what "no difference" is hiding. When agreement looked equal across groups, I tested the other end of the scale and found the gap.
  • I know what a sample can't support. Small groups were reported descriptively, not dressed up with significance tests.

Consulting work under a research consultancy's principals, who owned the client relationship and final report. The sample is self-selected (8% response) and unweighted; its racial mix roughly matched the campus population.

The question

Do experiences of campus differ by gender, race or disability, and where?

University leadership wanted a baseline on diversity, equity, inclusion and belonging before setting new strategy, covering bias, belonging, views of the institution and overall satisfaction.

Shape the instrument

Wording piped by role; every reported incident triggered "where" and "who" follow-ups.

Field it

Pretest and piping checks, then a one-week launch. Bounced addresses were fixed and re-sent.

Model it

Each question modeled by gender, race and disability at α = .01, cross-checked with a second specification.

Finding 1

Race and disability gaps ran through everything; gender gaps were rare

Race gaps showed up in bias, belonging and bottom-line views. The largest belonging gaps followed disability. Gender mattered mainly for views of the institution.

Finding 2

Agreement looked equal. Disagreement didn't.

On whether the university was diversifying its faculty, every group agreed at about the same rate. The difference hid in the neutral middle, so I tested the disagreeing end of the scale too.

Finding 3

The students who care most about representation see it least

Students of color were about four times as likely as White students to call it important to be taught by someone of their race or ethnicity, and far less likely to have been.

Rigor

The number I didn't test

What the raw tables showed
86% of senior administrators with a disability felt they couldn't be their authentic selves.
What I reported
The pattern, marked as descriptive.

That 86% is six of seven people. Outside students, groups were too small to test, so I showed the numbers without significance claims and flagged them for follow-up.

Looking back

What I'd do differently

  • Keep racial and ethnic groups separate where numbers allow. "People of color" merges very different experiences.
  • Model the full response scale with ordinal models, which would catch hidden disagreement by design.
  • Correct for multiple comparisons. The strict α helped, but there were well over 100 tests.
  • Check for nonresponse bias. At 8% response, compare early and late responders, or weight by role.
