Survey research · 2022

What parenthood costs a lawyer

A survey of more than 1,000 Seattle-area lawyers for the Mother Attorneys Mentoring Association of Seattle. It was designed to separate the career cost of being a mother from the cost of being a woman, and to show which firm policies could keep mothers in private practice.

Key strengths

What this case study shows about how I work

  • I design the survey around the analysis. Before we fielded, every question the client wanted answered was tied to specific survey items and a planned test, so the data could actually answer it.
  • I separate causes that travel together. Gender, parenthood and age are tangled in this population. A model that crosses gender with parenthood pulled them apart, and I left out a finding that turned out to be about age.
  • I turn a large set of tables into a few clear messages. I organized more than 60 results tables into a memo grouped by theme, then ran follow-up analyses as the client's questions got sharper. The published report and its recommendations drew on those results.

A consulting engagement. I worked under The Red Bee Group's principals, who wrote the final report: Mothers in Law Firms: Where Do We Go From Here? (MAMA Seattle, 2023). The sample is self-selected and results are unweighted.

The question

Why do mothers leave private practice, and what would keep them?

MAMA Seattle wanted first-hand evidence it could put in front of law firm leaders. That meant going beyond showing that women lawyers with children face obstacles: the survey had to say which obstacles come from parenting, which come from gender, and which firm policies lawyers believe would make a difference.

Approach

One survey, two paths, one analytical lens

Lawyers who had left private practice could answer questions that current private-practice lawyers couldn't, and the reverse was also true. So the survey began with a screening question and then sent each group down its own path.

Shape the instrument

The questionnaire went through many rounds of drafts with the client. I advised on wording, response scales and skip logic so each item could support a planned test.

Field it

I programmed and pretested the survey in Qualtrics and ran a three-week field period with a reminder, over two distribution channels.

Model it

We agreed on the analysis plan with the client before testing began. An R pipeline fit a model to every question and generated the tables for the memo.

The core design: gender × parenthood. Every outcome was modeled across all four groups, not just mothers versus everyone else. That separates effects of being a woman from effects of raising children, and it shows when the two interact, meaning when children affect women and men differently.

Each model matched its question's scale: logistic for yes/no and agree/disagree items, linear for ordered scales. Age was added as a control where it could confound the comparison.

Finding 1

Two different problems: a parenting load and a gender penalty

Some difficulties affected every parent, fathers included: work disrupted by family obligations, and lost sleep. Others affected women whether or not they had children, such as being seen as less competent, having less access to sponsors, and missing out on raises. A single "mothers vs. everyone" comparison would have mixed these two problems together, and they call for different fixes.

Finding 2

At home, mothers are still the default parent

These are lawyers in the same profession, often in dual-career households. Even so, mothers were far more likely than fathers to say they alone handle childcare and household tasks. Fathers more often described the same tasks as shared. The gap is widest for the planning work of arranging childcare.

Finding 3

Children push mothers' and fathers' careers in opposite directions

This is where the interaction model mattered most. Compared with men without children, fathers reported more billable hours and less interest in part-time work. Mothers' hours matched those of women without children, and their interest in part-time work was higher. Work/life balance was a bigger reason to stay for mothers than for other women, and a smaller one for fathers than for other men. Averaging across genders would have made these differences look small or absent.

Finding 4 · A follow-up analysis

What keeps mothers at a firm depends on the firm's size

After the first memo, the client asked a follow-up question, and I split mothers' reasons for staying by firm size. At small firms, mothers are held by time: manageable hours, caretaking flexibility and balance. At large firms, they are held by career: pay, advancement and colleagues. This became its own section of the published report, because a large firm and a small firm need different retention strategies.

Rigor

The finding I didn't report

Sometimes the most useful thing an analyst can do is stop a finding that isn't real before it reaches the report.

What the raw tables showed
Parents looked far more likely than non-parents to hold senior positions.

It looked like a strong claim that parenthood helps careers.

What the model showed
Age accounted for the gap.

Parents in the sample were older, and older lawyers are more senior. With age controlled, parenthood had no reliable effect on seniority, so the memo reported it that way.

From findings to action

Where firm policy and mothers' priorities diverge

Lawyers rated 28 possible firm strategies. Plotting how important each one is to mothers against how much more mothers value it than fathers do showed two kinds of strategy. Basics like paid leave and flexible hours are widely supported. Structural changes to how firms count work and give credit also matter to most mothers, but fathers rate them much lower. Those structural changes are where the case to firm leadership has to be made most strongly.

Looking back

What I'd do differently

  • Weight the sample. Respondents chose to take part, so people with strong feelings about the topic are probably overrepresented. Weighting to bar membership demographics would make the overall numbers more trustworthy.
  • Correct for multiple comparisons. The memo ran hundreds of tests at a .05 threshold, so some borderline results are likely false positives. I would have applied a false discovery rate correction and focused the report on the results that survive it.
  • Lead with effect sizes. Reporting gaps with confidence intervals, rather than p-values, would tell readers how big a difference is, not just whether it exists.
  • Plan for the survivorship problem. Lawyers who left private practice answered about their past, and those who stayed answered about their present. Following a group over time, even a small one, would show the order in which things actually happened.
