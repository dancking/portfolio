Measuring the Nobel Prize's gender gap

Of 881 Nobel laureates from 1901 to 2016, 47 were women. I broke that gap down by prize category, followed it across 113 award years and tested whether a recent uptick in female laureates is real or driven by a single unusual year.

Key Strengths

What this case study shows about how I work

  • I look past the headline number. Splitting the gap by category showed that the categories with narrower gaps simply awarded fewer prizes. Women weren't winning more of them.
  • I check whether a trend holds up before I report it. A possible recent rise in female laureates got a formal test, then a second test without the one unusual year that might have been driving it.
  • I design charts that make a pattern obvious. Shading every award year as all-male or not turns 113 data points into a picture that needs no explanation.

An exploratory analysis of a public dataset (TidyTuesday Nobel laureates). All figures are my originals, built in R.

The question

How big is the gap, and is it closing?

The first Nobel Prizes were awarded in 1901, 19 years before women in the US had the right to vote. I wanted to measure how large the gender gap among laureates is, where it is largest, and whether there is any sign that it is shrinking.

The data

Every Chemistry, Physics, Medicine, Economics, Literature and Peace laureate from 1901 through 2016. No prizes were awarded from 1940 to 1942, during the Nazi occupation of Norway, which leaves 113 award years.

Research questions

  1. How large is the gap, overall and by category?
  2. Has it changed over time?
  3. Is any recent change statistically meaningful?
  4. Where were male and female laureates born?
Approach

Count it, follow it over time, then test it

Count it

Totals by gender, then by category in a dumbbell chart, where the length of each line is the size of the gap.

Follow it over time

Yearly counts for women and men, the share of each year's prizes that went to men, and a smoothed trend line.

Test it

A linear model of the yearly share, refit without the most unusual year to see whether the result still holds.

Build 4

A possible turn at the end

A smoothed trend line sits near 100% men for most of the century, then dips over the final 15 or so years of the data.

That could be the start of a real change, or it could be noise. Answering that takes a statistical test.

Testing the trend

A real shift, or one unusual year?

To test whether women's share of prizes is rising, I fit a simple linear model to the yearly data. If it is, the slope b should be significant and negative: the proportion of male winners should be falling.

Proportion of male winners ~ Year

A negative, significant slope would mean the share of prizes going to men is shrinking over time.

All years
p = .06
b = −6: the model predicts the percentage of winners who are women rises by 6% each year.
Marginal
Excluding 2009
p = .17
b = −.02: a predicted .02% rise each year in the percentage of winners who are women.
Not significant

Why check 2009

A 6% yearly increase looked optimistic given the chart. In 2009, women made up an unusually large share of laureates (five winners, a record), so I refit the model without that year.

What it means

The trend doesn't hold up. Once a single year is removed, the model is no longer even marginally significant. The recent uptick is worth watching, but this data can't yet show that the gap is closing.
